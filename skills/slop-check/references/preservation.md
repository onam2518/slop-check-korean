# Voice and preservation

The edit starts with a preservation contract. Better rhythm is not a win if the rewrite drops a number, changes a claim, erases uncertainty, or turns the author into a generic professional. Over-editing is the most common failure of an anti-slop pass: it launders a real voice into the same flat prose it claims to fix.

## Preserve

- named entities, product names, places, people, organizations
- numbers, thresholds, dates, units, prices, percentages, versions
- URLs, citations, quotations, commands, identifiers, quoted terms
- causal claims and scope limits
- explicit uncertainty, probability, caveats, and modal verbs that carry meaning
- first-person stance when the author chose it
- personal details, mixed feelings, dry humor, deliberate unevenness

## Meaning checklist

For each paragraph:
1. What claims does the source make?
2. Which facts make those claims verifiable or specific?
3. Which qualifiers keep the claim honest?
4. Did the rewrite add an unsupported claim?
5. Did the rewrite make a narrow claim broad, or a tentative claim certain?

Critical failures block release: wrong number, changed entity, removed necessary qualifier, false causality, invented evidence, omitted required constraint.

## Evidence boundary

Concrete detail is an improvement only when the source licenses it. Before adding a specific, label it:
- `source`: present in the draft or supporting material
- `inference`: follows narrowly from the source, adds no new fact
- `placeholder`: useful example the user must verify (write it as `[bracketed]`)
- `unsafe`: plausible detail that would read as fact but is not supported

Only `source` and narrow `inference` go into final text. Leave `unsafe` out.

## Evidence-bound mode

Use when the draft is vague and no context is present. You may improve rhythm, remove hype, tighten claims, or call out missing evidence. You may not fill in the missing story.

Allowed: replace an inflated claim with a narrower one already in the source; say a claim needs proof instead of inventing it; use bracketed slots (`[specific deadline]`, `[owner]`, `[customer-visible change]`); ask for the facts.

Blocked, by copy type:
- support: invented teams, outages, invoices, deadlines, ownership; process promises ("we will review/follow up/resolve") the source does not state
- recruiting: invented employers, projects, levels, tools, candidate strengths
- incident: invented services, durations, failure modes, user impact, remediation; quality labels ("auditable", "secure", "resilient") not stated
- product: invented dashboards, tabs, integrations, analytics, metrics, outcomes
- strategy: invented plans, staffing, budgets, owners, governance

When evidence-bound mode leaves the text abstract, say so. The correct output can be a critique plus a request for missing facts. Density cannot come from fictional specificity.

## Slop or voice: validate before cutting

For each candidate, ask in order:
1. Does cutting it lose meaning? If yes, it may be content. Keep or reword.
2. Is it formula or choice? Would the author defend it, or did a pattern put it there?
3. Does it announce rather than deliver? Meta-commentary is almost always cuttable.
4. Does it add heat or light? Cut heat, keep light.
5. Would a skeptical reader trust the sentence more or less without it?

Survives all five: leave it.

## What to keep (the over-correction guard)

Earned fragments ("Not flagged after the fact. Removed."); deliberate parallelism ("You can have either. You cannot have both."); a strong closing line; first-person conviction ("We are not comfortable saying..."); profanity, humor, blunt language, self-interruptions, honest admissions that belong to the writer. The test: could the author defend this choice if asked? If yes, keep it. Slop cannot be defended.

## Control samples

Already-good human prose should often get a light edit or none. Preserve precise technical density, formal-but-human academic prose, clear non-native English choices, and lyrical passages where polish is the point. If the rewrite changes more than a fifth of a control sample without a clear reason, treat it as a warning.

## Voice matching

When the user supplies a writing sample, it outranks every style rule here, including the em dash rule. Read it first; note sentence length, vocabulary, paragraph openings, punctuation, recurring phrases, transitions. Match those habits. Do not upgrade casual words, regularize quirks, or add slang, typos, contractions, jokes, or confessions the sample does not support.

## Calibration by register

- Light touch for a personal blog or a strong-voiced author: fix clear formula and lexicon only; protect voice aggressively.
- Heavier touch for corporate or marketing copy, which is slop-dense: still surgical, but more candidates are real.
- Match register: a playful piece stays playful; a white paper stays measured. Do not neutralize tone toward a generic middle.
